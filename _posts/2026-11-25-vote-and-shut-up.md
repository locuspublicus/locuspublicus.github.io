Every four years, someone hands you a pencil.

You walk into a school gym that smells of floor polish and old sandwiches, you step behind a little cardboard wall, and you make a cross. Then you fold the paper, drop it in a box, and go home. Congratulations. You have just exercised the sacred right that generations fought and died for.

Now shut up until next time.

That's the deal. That's the whole deal. And we've been sold it so well, for so long, that most of us confuse the pencil with the power.

I'm not here to tell you democracy is bad. It isn't. Compared with kings, juntas, and men who name themselves "Supreme Leader," it's a miracle. I'd rather argue with a parliament than disappear into a basement. But "better than a dictatorship" is a remarkably low bar, and we've been limboing under it for about two hundred years while calling it a high jump.

So let's look at what we actually have. And at what the people who invented the word had in mind.

## The Athenians would have called us an oligarchy

Around 508 BC, Athens did something weird. It decided that ordinary citizens should run the city. Not "choose people to run it." *Run it.*

The core was the **Ekklesia**, the assembly, where every citizen could show up, speak, and vote directly on laws, war, peace, and money. In the fourth century, the assembly met around forty times a year, and certain decisions needed a quorum of six thousand people.[^hansen]

The agenda was prepared by the **Boule**, a council of five hundred. They weren't elected. They were drawn **by lot**, fifty from each of the ten tribes, for one year, and you could serve at most twice in your life.[^athpol] Most of the city's roughly seven hundred officials were also chosen by lot.[^athpol] The big jury courts? Lot again, hundreds of citizens at a time, paid a daily wage so that a fisherman could afford to judge a rich man.

And here's the part that should make every modern politician sweat: at the end of their term, every official faced the **euthyna**, a formal audit. You had to account for the money you touched and the decisions you made. If you'd screwed up or stolen, you could be fined, prosecuted, or worse.[^athpol] Athens also had ostracism, which let the assembly exile anyone who was getting too powerful for ten years, no crime required.

Aristotle, who wasn't even a fan, put it bluntly: choosing officials by lot is democratic, choosing them by **election** is *oligarchic*.[^politics] Elections, in his view, favour the rich, the famous, and the well-connected. Which, when you look at any parliament on earth, is less a theory than a photograph.

Now, before anybody builds a temple to Pericles: Athenian democracy was also a club with a brutal door policy. **Women: out. Slaves: out. Metics,** the resident foreigners who worked, paid taxes, and fought in wars: **out.** Of maybe a quarter of a million people in Attica, only around thirty thousand adult men had a vote.[^hansen] It was direct democracy for a minority, built on the forced labour of others.

So no, Athens isn't the answer. But it asked a better question than we do. They asked: *how do we stop power from settling into a class?* We ask: *which class would you like this time?*

## Who you never voted for

Here's a fun game. Make a list of the people who shape your life the most. Then cross out everyone whose name was ever on your ballot.

The list doesn't get much shorter.

- **The European Commission** proposes almost all EU legislation. Its members are nominated by national governments and confirmed by the European Parliament as a package.[^teu] Nobody in Europe has ever voted for "their" commissioner.
- **The European Central Bank** sets the interest rate on your mortgage, your savings, and your country's debt. It is independent by treaty, explicitly shielded from instructions by any government.[^tfeu] There are good reasons for that. But "good reasons" is not the same as "you have a say."
- **Constitutional judges.** In Germany, the sixteen judges of the Federal Constitutional Court are chosen by two-thirds majorities in the Bundestag and Bundesrat for a single twelve-year term.[^bverfgg] They decide what the elected parliament may do. You don't decide who they are.
- **The ministerial bureaucracy.** Ministers come and go. The senior civil servants who write the drafts, know the files, and decide which options ever reach the minister's desk stay for decades. In Germany, many of them are tenured for life.

And what about the decisions themselves? Germany's Basic Law allows a nationwide referendum in exactly two cases: when the borders of the federal states are redrawn, and if the country ever adopts a new constitution.[^gg] Everything else, from pensions to wars to the energy system, is decided *for* you. Switzerland, by contrast, holds national votes several times a year and has done so since the nineteenth century. Switzerland has not collapsed. Its cheese is fine.

Then there's the uncomfortable research. In 2014, the political scientists Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page analysed nearly 1,800 policy decisions in the United States and compared them with what different groups wanted. Their finding: once you account for what economic elites and organised interest groups want, the preferences of average citizens have **close to zero** independent effect on what becomes policy.[^gilens] That's the US, and the study has its critics. But ask yourself honestly whether you'd bet your rent that Europe looks completely different.

So this is the sale that's been running for centuries: *the vote is your voice.* In reality, the vote is a hiring decision for a job you can't supervise, filled by someone you can only fire four years later, if the alternatives aren't worse.

## The engine inside the machine

Democracy is the house. Capitalism is the furnace in the basement. And to be fair, for a while that furnace kept everyone warm.

After 1945, West Germany got what they called the **Wirtschaftswunder**, the economic miracle. Within two decades, a bombed-out country had full employment, a growing middle class, and a welfare state that was the envy of the world. The deal was called the "social market economy": markets create wealth, the state makes sure enough of it reaches the people who do the work. Across the West, the same decades brought cars, fridges, holidays, pensions, and the radical idea that a worker's kid could go to university.

That happened. I'm not going to pretend it didn't. Capitalism, fenced in by strong rules, unions, and taxes, produced more material prosperity for more people than any system before it.

But somewhere around the 1980s, the fence started coming down, plank by plank, and the furnace started heating only the upper floors.

Today, the richest tenth of households in Germany own **more than half** of all private net wealth, while the poorer half owns only a few percent.[^bundesbank] Globally, the top ten percent hold about three-quarters of all wealth.[^wir] In Germany, roughly one in five jobs counts as low-wage.[^destatis] Your postcode and your parents' bank account predict your future better than your talent does. That's what classism is: not a rude word for snobs, but a system where where you start decides where you end.

And the welfare state? It isn't being abolished. It's being starved. Slowly, quietly, with spreadsheets. The pension level has been cut. Schools leak. Bridges close. Trains are late so often it's become a national comedy genre. Municipalities report a public investment backlog in the hundreds of billions.[^kfw] Not because a villain planned it, but because the rules of the game reward short-term profit over long-term substance, and the people who write the rules have every incentive to keep it that way.

## Nobody gets fired

Here's what really gets me.

If you run a bakery and you burn every loaf for four years, you're out of business. If you're a nurse and you mix up medication, you face consequences. If you're a bus driver and you crash the bus, you're no longer a bus driver.

If you're a minister and you crash the bus? You get a book deal.

Germany planned a car toll on its motorways. The minister in charge signed the operator contracts before the European Court of Justice had ruled on whether the toll was even legal. It wasn't. The court killed it in 2019. An arbitration panel later awarded the operators **243 million euros** in damages, paid by taxpayers.[^maut] The ministry examined whether to hold the former minister personally liable and decided not to sue.[^lto]

Berlin's new airport was supposed to open in 2011. It opened in October 2020, nine years late, at roughly three times the original budget.[^ber] Tell me who lost their pension over that.

This isn't about one party. It's structural. There are no **KPIs** in politics. No measurable goals agreed at the start, no review at the end, no consequence for missing them. The only accountability mechanism is the next election, and by then the voters have been distracted by three new scandals, a culture war, and a football tournament.

Athens had the euthyna. We have a talk show.

## Three ideas for a system that answers back

So what now? Burn it down? No. I've seen what's left after people burn things down: usually a new guy with a bigger palace.

What I want is renewal. A system that remembers it works for us. Here are three proposals. You don't have to like them. You just have to think about them.

### 1. Govern together, deliver together, or step aside

Right now, parliament is a permanent election campaign. The governing party governs, the opposition opposes, and both spend four years trying to make the other side look bad. If the government succeeds, the opposition loses. So the opposition has every reason to want the country to fail a little. Think about that.

Here's a different model:

- **The winning party sets the core.** It gets a clear mandate to drive a defined set of reforms and continue key policies. That's what the election is for.
- **The big questions go to the people.** Alongside that core, specific issues of major importance are put to binding referendums during the term, properly prepared, with real information, not slogans.
- **Everyone else has a duty to contribute.** Opposition parties aren't spectators. Once goals are set, by the majority's mandate and by the people's votes, *all* parties are obliged to work toward them, in committees, in drafting, in implementation. You can disagree about the how. You can't sabotage the what.
- **Goals come with numbers and deadlines.** At the start of the term, measurable targets are fixed and published: housing units, train punctuality, waiting times for doctor appointments, emissions, whatever was promised.
- **Miss them, and you're out.** Representatives who were responsible for a goal and failed to meet it within the agreed timeframe are barred from standing for office in the next term, judged at the end of the legislative period or in a review shortly afterward.

Yes, this has sharp edges, and I'd rather name them than hide them. Who sets the targets, so that no one games them by promising nothing? An independent body, think of an audit court combined with a citizens' panel, not the politicians themselves. What about failures caused by a pandemic or a war? Those need a documented exception, decided by that same independent body, not by the person who failed. And responsibility has to be personal and traceable, so an opposition MP isn't punished for a minister's mess, only for blocking work they were obliged to do.

But the core principle is ancient and simple: **whoever takes public power owes an account of how they used it.** The Athenians did it 2,500 years ago, without spreadsheets.

### 2. Five years for the common good

Imagine every citizen, at some point in their life, serves the state for **five years**. Not necessarily in uniform. In hospitals, in schools, in elderly care, in disaster protection, in climate adaptation, in digital administration, in local government, or yes, in defence. Regionally or nationwide, wherever they're needed and wherever their skills fit.

And not as a punishment, but on **attractive terms**: a real salary, extra pension credits, free or discounted university or vocational training afterward, priority for public housing, recognised qualifications that count on the job market.

Why five years? Because a few months of service just produces tourists. Five years produces people who understand how the system actually works, where it breaks, and why. They'd come back into private life knowing what a ward looks like at 3 a.m., what a municipal budget can and can't do, how slow a permit is from the inside. Fewer people would fall for easy slogans. More would know how to fix things.

Five years is long, I know. It doesn't have to be one block. It could be split, two years after school, the rest later in life, so a forty-five-year-old engineer can spend a year helping a town rebuild its water system.

Germany suspended compulsory military service in 2011 and has only just started bringing a new, still voluntary, service model back since January 2026, with a mandatory questionnaire for young men.[^wehrdienst] The debate is open. So let's make it bigger than the army. A society that asks nothing of its members gets members who expect nothing of it.

### 3. Bring back the lottery

My own addition, straight from Athens: a **citizens' chamber drawn by lot**.

A few hundred people, randomly selected and weighted so they mirror the population by age, gender, region, and income. They serve for a limited time, are paid properly, and receive briefings from experts on all sides. Their job: set part of the political agenda, review major legislation, and decide which issues go to a referendum.

Sounds utopian? It has been done. Ireland let randomly selected citizens deliberate on marriage equality and abortion, two questions politicians had avoided for decades. The assemblies recommended change, the country voted, and in 2015 and 2018 Ireland's constitution changed with clear majorities.[^ireland] The German-speaking community of Belgium has had a permanent citizens' council, drawn by lot, built into its parliament since 2019.[^ostbelgien]

Randomly selected people have no donors to please, no career to protect, no party to obey. They're the closest thing we have to the actual public. Aristotle would approve. Your average lobbyist would hate it. That alone is a recommendation.

## Pull up a chair

None of this will be handed to us. Systems don't reform themselves out of kindness. The people who benefit from the current setup will tell you it's naive, dangerous, impractical. They told the Athenians that. They told the people who wanted universal suffrage that. They told women who wanted the vote that.

Here's what I believe. Democracy isn't a thing you have. It's a thing you do. And right now, most of us are allowed to do it for about ninety seconds every four years.

We deserve more than a pencil. And they deserve more than a free pass.

You don't have to agree with me. You just have to think.

*Hakbard*
