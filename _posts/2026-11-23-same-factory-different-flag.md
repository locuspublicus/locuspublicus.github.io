Every time a Western news anchor says "Communist China," listen to the tone. It's not the tone of analysis. It's the tone your neighbour uses for the guy who never sorts his recycling. Moral disgust with a weather map behind it.

So let's ask the rude question nobody on the evening news asks: is this actually communism? Or have we just decided it has to be, because a villain is more useful than a mirror?

## The communism that sells you a phone

Here is a "communist" country for you. In 2024, China had more dollar billionaires than the United States: 814 to 800.[^1] Its official income Gini coefficient – the standard measure of inequality, where 0 means everyone has the same and 1 means one guy has everything – sat at 0.467 in 2022. A leading Chinese economist puts the wealth Gini above 0.7.[^2] By most measures, China is *more* unequal than the United States, which is itself the most unequal country in the rich world.[^3]

Vietnam calls its system a "socialist-oriented market economy." Since the Doi Moi reforms of 1986, it has handed farmland back to households, opened up to foreign trade and become the place your sneakers come from.[^4]

Karl Marx would not recognise any of this. A Communist Party running one of the most aggressive capitalisms on earth isn't communism. It's a red logo on a very familiar business model.

So why do we keep using the word? Because "the world's other great capitalist superpower" doesn't make you afraid. And fear sells better than nuance. Always has.

## The numbers that don't make the evening news

Now for the part that makes people uncomfortable at dinner.

Over four decades, the number of Chinese living below the World Bank's extreme poverty line fell by close to 800 million. That is roughly three quarters of the entire global reduction in extreme poverty over the same period.[^5] Not exactly the work of a regime with nothing to show for itself. A World Bank president once called it "one of the great stories in human history."[^6]

Vietnam: extreme poverty fell from around 45 percent of the population in 1992 to under 1 percent in 2022. Infant mortality more than halved. National health insurance now covers 93 percent of people.[^7]

Shelter, food, water, healthcare. Remember the list of fundamentals I keep banging on about on this blog? Hundreds of millions of people got a lot closer to that list in a single lifetime. You can hate the party and still respect the fact.

What about happiness? Let me be honest here, because this is where it would be easy to cheat. In the 2026 World Happiness Report, Vietnam ranks 45th, China 65th – and the United States 23rd.[^8] So no, the average Chinese person is not happier than the average American. But here's the trend that matters: China and Vietnam are among the countries with the biggest gains in life satisfaction since the late 2000s,[^9] while happiness in most Western countries keeps sliding.[^10] They're climbing a ladder. We're slowly falling off ours and blaming the guy below.

Trust tells a similar story. In the Edelman Trust Barometer, China sits at the very top of the trust index, with around 77 to 80 percent of people trusting their institutions. The United States sits at 47. Germany at 41.[^11]

Grain of salt? Absolutely. People in a one-party state don't always tell pollsters what they really think of the one party. But it's a grain, not a sack. You don't fake a 40-year trend line that big with fear alone.

And the rest of the world seems to be noticing. In the 2026 Democracy Perception Index, nearly 100,000 people across 98 countries rated the US at a net score of minus 16. China came in at plus 7. Even Russia scored better than America.[^12] Respondents now count the United States among the biggest threats to world stability.[^13]

## Big Brother has a marketing department

Ah, but surveillance! The social credit score! The dystopia where you lose points for jaywalking!

Here's the funny thing. That score – the single number deciding your fate – doesn't exist. Researchers who have spent years on this describe China's "social credit system" as a fragmented, barely digitised patchwork of blacklists that mostly targets *companies*: tax fraud, unpaid court judgments, food safety.[^14] Western media turned a bureaucratic mess into Black Mirror because Black Mirror gets more clicks.

And guess where the idea came from in the first place. According to the Mercator Institute for China Studies, the system was originally inspired by credit scoring in the West – FICO in the United States and Schufa in Germany.[^15] We exported the score, then got outraged when they copied it.

Meanwhile, at home: in 2020, a US federal appeals court ruled that the NSA's mass collection of Americans' phone records – the program Edward Snowden exposed – was unlawful, and that the officials who defended it hadn't told the truth.[^16] And the market does the rest. Global advertising spending is forecast to hit 1.34 trillion dollars in 2026. Per person, advertisers spend about 1,395 dollars a year on every American, compared with about 170 dollars on every Chinese citizen.[^17] Nobody in Washington needs a social credit score. They have a credit score, a FICO number, a recommendation algorithm and an ad budget the size of a mid-sized country.

Now the honest part, because I promised you honesty and not a Beijing press release: China *does* run the largest camera network on the planet. More than half of the world's surveillance cameras are in China, somewhere around 700 million of them.[^18] That's not Western propaganda. That's hardware.

So no, it's not the same. It's two different flavours of the same thing. In China, the state watches you. In the West, the market watches you – and the state borrows the footage when it feels like it.

## The body count problem

The easy, lazy version of this post would now say: "The West has caused far more suffering." I wanted to write that sentence. The numbers wouldn't let me.

Look at our side of the ledger first. The Vietnam War killed somewhere around two million Vietnamese, with some estimates going as high as 3.8 million.[^19] Between 2.1 and 4.8 million Vietnamese people were exposed to Agent Orange, and to this day no one has been held legally accountable for it.[^20] The post-9/11 wars in Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria, Yemen and Pakistan have caused an estimated 4.5 to 4.7 million deaths, directly and indirectly.[^21]

Now their side. Mao's Great Leap Forward caused the largest famine in human history. Estimates range from 15 to 55 million dead; the most credible put it at 35 to 45 million.[^22] There was Tiananmen in 1989. And in 2022, the UN Human Rights Office concluded that China's mass detention of Uyghurs and other Muslim minorities in Xinjiang may constitute crimes against humanity.[^23]

So no – the scoreboard doesn't acquit the Communist Party. Nobody walks out of this comparison clean.

But here's the double standard I *can* prove: who gets to count. In our media, their dead are crimes, with names and anniversaries. Our dead are "collateral damage," with a footnote. Agent Orange gets a documentary. The Great Leap gets a moral lesson. Same horror, different editing.

## They built it on our shopping carts

None of this is a story of two separate worlds. China didn't rise despite us. It rose *because* of us – our consumption, our outsourcing, our insatiable appetite for cheap stuff we don't need.

Yanis Varoufakis, Greece's former finance minister and no fan of the Chinese Communist Party, likes to point out how tightly the two are wired together. China bought a huge chunk of American debt so Americans could keep buying Chinese products. And in 2008, when Western finance blew itself up, it was Beijing that pumped up a massive credit boom to keep the global economy afloat. Without that, Varoufakis told an American audience, "you would be in a new Great Depression now."[^24]

They make. We buy. They lend us the money to keep buying. That's not a Cold War. That's a codependent marriage with nukes.

And it goes deeper. In *Technofeudalism*, Varoufakis argues that power no longer sits with the owners of factories, but with the owners of what he calls cloud capital – the platforms, algorithms and networks that shape what we want and take a cut of everything we buy. And: "Only two countries have spawned large quantities of cloud capital."[^25] The US and China. The great clash of civilisations is, at its core, two landlords fighting over the same tenants. Us.

Even inside China, some Marxist economists are asking the question we should be asking here: whether pushing people to consume more can ever be anything but a treadmill that runs faster and goes nowhere.[^26] Funny. That's the same question this blog keeps asking about us.

## So what now?

Here's where I land.

China and Vietnam delivered some of the basics – food, shelter, healthcare, a way out of poverty – at a scale and speed the world has never seen. They did it while denying people the right to say out loud what they think of those who run the show.

The West delivered the right to say what you think. And it's increasingly failing at the basics: housing you can afford, trust in institutions, the sense that your kids will have it better than you.

Neither is the answer. And if we're honest, we're not that different anymore. Same factory. Same supply chains. Same algorithms. Same billionaires. Different flags.

Which means the responsibility is shared too. We don't get to point at Beijing and feel clean. If we're all running the same machine under different colours, then building a better one – one that delivers enough instead of more, freedom instead of luxury – is everybody's job.

We keep staring at China like it's an enemy. Maybe what really scares us is that it's a mirror.

## Where I might be wrong

You don't have to agree with me, so here's the case against this post, as its sharpest critics would make it:

- **Polls in one-party states are unreliable.** When criticising the government can cost you your job or your freedom, "high trust" might just mean "high fear." The happiness and trust data could be flattering China more than it deserves.
- **The free press is the difference.** We know about the NSA, Agent Orange and Iraq precisely because Western journalists, courts and whistleblowers could expose them. In China, the Great Leap and Tiananmen are still censored. Self-correction isn't a small detail; it might be the whole point.
- **The success came from markets, not communism.** China's and Vietnam's poverty miracles started when they *abandoned* central planning and opened up to trade and private enterprise. You could read this as a victory for capitalism, not a defence of red flags.
- **The threat is real.** Taiwan, Hong Kong, the South China Sea and Xinjiang are not inventions of Fox News. Calling Western concern "propaganda" can mean ignoring people whose freedom is actually on the line.

I take every one of those seriously. I just don't think they let us off the hook.

Pull up a chair. Argue with me.

– Hakbard
