Start with a white T-shirt. Not a fancy one. Organic cotton, knitted in Germany on circular machines that are older than most people's grandparents, no side seams, built to survive a decade of washing. It costs about €85. Another loopwheeled version costs €105. It is, by every reasonable standard, a better object than the four-pack of polyester-blend tees you can buy for the price of a sandwich.

Now hold it next to a different number. In 2019, the median human being on this planet lived on about US$7.60 a day. Forty-seven percent of humanity lived on less than $6.85.[^1] For half the species, that good white T-shirt is not a purchase. It is a week and a half of everything: food, rent, bus fare, medicine.

<figure class="grafik">{% include grafiken/tage-medianeinkommen.svg %}<figcaption>Calculation based on Asket’s US price and the World Bank’s 2019 global median income.</figcaption></figure>

That is the uncomfortable center of what I want to talk about. A new generation of clothing brands has made quality, longevity and honesty fashionable again. And the moment you love what they make, you run into a question they rarely ask out loud: *who is this world actually for?*

## The new classics

Over the last fifteen years, a quiet counter-movement has grown in the shadow of fast fashion. Merz b. Schwanen revived a Swabian knitwear manufactory founded in 1911 after a Berlin couple found a ninety-year-old henley from the old firm at a flea market.[^2] Asket in Stockholm sells a "permanent collection" with no seasons and publishes the cost breakdown and the factories behind every garment.[^3] About Companions in Berlin offers repair guarantees and makes its pieces with European specialists.[^4] Across the oceans, labels like Robert Barakett in Montreal, the Japanese knitwear brand Mooncastle and the small-batch sock maker Knestknit sell what the trade press calls "elevated basics."[^5]

Let's be fair before we get angry. These brands are answering a real catastrophe. According to the Ellen MacArthur Foundation, the number of times a garment is worn before it's thrown away fell by 36 percent in fifteen years. Every second, a garbage truck's worth of textiles is landfilled or burned. Less than one percent of clothing material is ever recycled into new clothing.[^6] Against that backdrop, a company that says *buy less, buy better, keep it longer* is not the villain. It's one of the few adults in the room.

So the problem is not the shirt. The problem is the door.

## Captain Vimes goes shopping

In his 1993 novel *Men at Arms*, Terry Pratchett gave the economics of being poor its most famous explanation. His character Sam Vimes, a city watchman, notes that a good pair of boots costs fifty dollars and lasts for years, while cheap boots cost ten and fall apart in a season or two. Over a decade the poor man pays twice as much and still has wet feet.[^7] In 2022, the Pratchett estate backed a "Vimes Boots" index proposed by the British food writer Jack Monroe to measure exactly this hidden tax on poverty.[^7]

Here's the irony. The entire sales pitch of slow fashion *is* the boots theory. Cost per wear. Buy once, buy right. Every product page of every heritage brand is basically Sam Vimes with better lighting.

But Vimes never said the boots theory was a lifestyle choice. He said it was a trap. The man with ten dollars is not making a poor decision. He's making the only decision his wallet allows. Telling him that the fifty-dollar boots are cheaper *in the long run* is like telling a drowning man that swimming lessons are a great investment.

Sustainability, as currently sold, requires up-front capital. That turns a moral virtue into a financial privilege. And once virtue has a price tag, it becomes something else entirely: a marker of class.

## What a T-shirt actually costs

We don't have to guess how much of a premium T-shirt's price is the shirt. One of these brands tells us.

Asket sells its standard organic cotton T-shirt for US$65 in its US store. According to its own published breakdown, the garment costs €11.10 to make and deliver: €4.20 for the fabric, €6.30 for labour, €0.40 for trims and €0.30 for transport. It's knitted, dyed, cut and sewn in northern Portugal, and Asket names each facility.[^3]


<figure class="grafik">{% include grafiken/kosten-tshirt.svg %}<figcaption>Source: Asket’s published cost breakdown for its standard T-shirt. Components are rounded.</figcaption></figure>

This is genuinely admirable transparency, and it deserves credit precisely because it lets us do the math. The finished shirt costs roughly a sixth of its retail price. The rest pays for design, overhead, marketing, retail, returns, taxes and margin. None of that is a scandal. Small companies with small volumes and honest suppliers are expensive to run.

But it does reveal something important: **the price of the "good" shirt is not dictated by the cost of doing good.** Paying the Portuguese workers who sewed it more generously would add a few euros, not fifty. The gap between €11 and $65 is not the price of ethics. It's mostly the price of being a brand.

And for most heritage labels, we don't get a breakdown at all. We get a story: the old machines, the founder's grandfather, the flea-market henley. The story is lovely. It's also what you're paying for.

## The aristocracy of restraint

In 1899, the economist Thorstein Veblen coined the term *conspicuous consumption* to describe how the rich display status by spending visibly and wastefully.[^8] A century later, the sociologist Elizabeth Currid-Halkett documented something subtler. Today's educated upper-middle class signals status through *inconspicuous* consumption: organic food, education, wellness, and products whose value is legible only to people who already know what to look for.[^9]

Pierre Bourdieu would have recognized the move instantly. In *Distinction* (1979), he argued that taste is never neutral; it's one of the ways social classes recognize each other and keep each other out.[^10]

Slow fashion fits this pattern almost perfectly. A loopwheeled T-shirt doesn't shout. It has no logo. To most people it looks exactly like a cheap white T-shirt. Only the initiated know the difference, and *that's the point*. The new classics have turned restraint itself into a luxury good. Fast fashion lets the poor imitate the rich. Slow fashion lets the rich distinguish themselves from people who imitate them, while feeling good about the planet.

That's not hypocrisy yet. That's sociology. Hypocrisy needs one more ingredient.

## Where conscience becomes hypocrisy

A brand that makes excellent clothes for wealthy people and says so is not a hypocrite. It's a shop. Several of the labels I mentioned, as far as I can find, don't even make big sustainability claims. They sell quality. Fine.

Hypocrisy starts the moment a company sells *change* and delivers *distinction*. When "sustainability" appears in every second sentence of the marketing, but the business model only works if the customer belongs to the global top few percent. When a brand talks about saving the planet while the planet's majority can't afford a sleeve.

And the industry's track record on its own workers makes the gap obvious. In September 2026, the Clean Clothes Campaign's Fashion Checker assessed 110 major fashion and footwear brands. About a third had publicly committed to living wages. Four percent showed tangible progress. Only three percent published the wages paid in their factories. Garment workers in Bangladesh and Pakistan earn over 70 percent less than a living wage.[^11] That survey covers big brands, not small heritage labels. But it tells you how rare it is for anyone in fashion, large or small, to *prove* their ethics instead of merely narrating them.

So when I'm asked whether these founders are idealists or just people with a beautiful hobby, a comfortable income and a good story, my honest answer is: *I don't know, and neither do you, because most of them don't give us the data to decide.* That opacity is itself the tell.

## The Patagonia benchmark

People like to hold up Patagonia as proof that a clothing company can be more than a mirror for its customers' self-image. In September 2022, Yvon Chouinard transferred the company: all voting stock (2 percent) went to the Patagonia Purpose Trust, all non-voting stock (98 percent) to the Holdfast Collective, a nonprofit that receives every dollar of profit not reinvested in the business, roughly $100 million a year, to fight the climate crisis. "Earth is now our only shareholder," the company announced.[^12]

It's a remarkable act, and it isn't spotless. Critics pointed out that structuring the gift through a 501(c)(4) organization spared the family a tax bill that could have exceeded $700 million in capital gains, at the cost of about $17.5 million in gift tax, and that such organizations can spend unlimited money on politics.[^13]

But notice what Patagonia did *not* do. It didn't make its jackets cheap. A Patagonia fleece is still a middle-class purchase. What Patagonia changed was not the price at the door. It changed **where the money goes after it walks through**.

That's the real benchmark, and it's a fairer one than "be affordable." No small brand paying fair European wages can sell shirts at fast-fashion prices. But every brand can answer a few questions:

1. **Where does the profit go?** Into the founder's lifestyle, into growth, or into something beyond the company?
2. **Can you prove your ethics?** Published factory lists, cost breakdowns and actual wages, not adjectives.
3. **Do you widen the door over time?** Repair services, resale programs, seconds, take-back schemes that let a second, cheaper owner enjoy the same shirt.
4. **Do you use your voice?** Do you advocate for higher wages, fairer taxes and stronger supply-chain laws, or do you sell individual virtue as a substitute for political change?

A brand that scores well on these is doing something. A brand that scores zero while spending its marketing budget on the word "conscious" is selling indulgences.

## It was never going to be the shirt

Let me be clear about the part no T-shirt can fix. That half of humanity can't afford a good shirt is not a fashion problem. It's a problem of wages, taxes, ownership and power. Purchasing power is political. It's set by labor law, by who captures the gains of productivity, by whether economies are run for the many or for the shareholders. Markets can be powerful tools for good, but only inside rules that make them serve people. Asking boutique brands to solve global inequality is like asking a candle to heat the house.

But that cuts both ways. If purchasing power is political, then marketing ethics as a *shopping decision* is part of the problem. Every campaign that tells us we can change the world by choosing the right T-shirt quietly tells us we don't need to change anything else. It turns citizens into customers and solidarity into a checkout button.

So here's my modest proposal, from a guy who also owns a couple of very nice shirts.

The most sustainable garment on Earth is the one already in your closet. The second most sustainable is the one in a stranger's closet, available secondhand. And the most ethical brand is not the one with the best story. It's the one that shows you its numbers, shares its profits and argues as loudly for a fair wage as it does for organic cotton.

Everything else is just a very well-made white T-shirt. Enjoy it. Just don't mistake it for a revolution.
